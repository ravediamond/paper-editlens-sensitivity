Most robot-learning papers are tested in simulation or on rigs above $20k: 16 out of 22 papers, and only two were tested on rigs under $5k. But does this work on low-cost hardware? That's what small teams want to know. So what we did is test 37 claims from 22 robot-learning papers. Our rig was an SO-101 arm and a Jetson Orin NX with 16 GB of memory, for a total of €1,350 and just $45 of compute. We picked papers from the stack we run: world models, VLAs, failure monitors, chunking and edge latency. We use two rules for every test: the bar is committed in Git before testing, and the cheapest baseline is run first, on the same rows (copy-last, commanded joints, episode length). The result was very surprising. Only 12 out of 37 claims hold, and 18 fail. This is a surprise because several methods lose to that dumb baseline, not just to the bar. We distinguish three ways these papers fail. The first is being too slow: adaptive chunking, for example, takes nearly 5 seconds on the Orin, which has a budget of 0.3 seconds. Then you have the judges that lose to episode length: the reward model reaches an AUROC of 0.82, but the length alone reaches 0.94. And then the world-model latents lose to the commanded joints and an action-only baseline. So the advice: always start a re-test from the cheapest baseline. We also checked our Git history: no bar was written after its result. Everything is public, and every number can be reproduced from the repo.
